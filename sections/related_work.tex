\subsection{Statistical Time-Series Modeling}

Vegetation indices such as NDVI collected over years from platforms such as Sentinel-2 and Landsat are widely used to quantify vegetation condition from optical satellite imagery and have served as a proxy for canopy greenness and structural health. Lasaponara et al.~\cite{lasaponara2024} demonstrate how dense Sentinel-2 NDVI records can be segmented into stable periods and change points using a continuous monitoring framework. By identifying moments when the time series departs from its expected seasonal behavior and fitting trend models to those segments, their approach captures both the timing and magnitude of vegetation stress linked to pest activity. Likewise, Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang_aewma2024} construct a seasonal model of NDVI patterns and then monitor departures from this expected curve using an Adaptive Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (AEWMA) approach that emphasizes persistent deviations rather than short-term noise. Their method detects both sudden disturbances such as fire or harvest, and gradual canopy decline caused by insect damage or degradation.

The BFAST model~\cite{kennedy2010} decomposes vegetation-index time series into trend, seasonal, and remainder components and identifies structural breaks that may indicate disturbance. This is useful for forests because seasonal cycles often hide early stress signals. A near-real-time anomaly framework~\cite{xie2025} was also proposed which builds long-term baselines for vegetation indices and converts deviations into standardized anomaly scores using a small set of greenness and moisture indices, aiming to work across different forest types. Together, these studies show that vegetation stress can be identified by first establishing a historical seasonal baseline and then systematically monitoring departures from that baseline over time—a principle that directly informs our Z-score detection approach.

\subsection{Spectral and Species-Specific Approaches}

Some studies argue that generic vegetation indices are not sufficient because different forest species exhibit stress responses in different spectral regions and at different temporal scales. A kernel-based early detection framework for bark beetle infestation~\cite{jamali2024} uses dense Sentinel-2 vegetation index time series and integrates temporal behavior with local spatial context to generate anomaly scores. By modeling subtle deviations in spectral trajectories, the method is able to identify early infestation signals prior to visible crown discoloration, capturing gradual low-magnitude spectral shifts that purely structural breakpoint approaches miss.

Carletti et al.~\cite{carletti2025} further demonstrate that species-specific spectral responses must be considered when designing detection frameworks. Their analysis of Sentinel-2 time series across conifer species shows that red-edge and water-sensitive indices behave differently depending on tree species and environmental conditions, and that longer temporal windows are often required to stabilize early-warning indicators. König et al.~\cite{konig2023} find that Sentinel-2 red-edge (CRE) and SWIR-based (NBR) indices achieve higher spatial accuracy in spruce forests by targeting chlorophyll and moisture loss. Their results suggest that spectral sensitivity to specific physiological traits is more vital for early detection than increased temporal density from multi-sensor fusion, and they highlight the inadequacy of C-band SAR for identifying the low-magnitude structural shifts present in early infestations. These studies motivate our use of both NDVI (greenness) and NDMI (moisture) as complementary indices rather than relying on a single spectral signal.



\subsection{Geospatial Big-Data Systems and Monitoring Pipelines}

Ghilardi et al.~\cite{ghilardi2025} compared seven global satellite data products in Madagascar and found substantial disagreement in forest change detection, particularly in drier ecoregions, underscoring the need for harmonized, adaptable systems. The space-time cube model~\cite{verbesselt2010} organizes raster time series into a multidimensional structure indexed along the temporal axis, enabling fast retrieval of pixel histories and supporting early detection pipelines that repeatedly query long time series for millions of pixels. RDPro~\cite{rdpro2024} introduces the ``Maplet'' model, which partitions large raster datasets into small tiles for direct distributed processing, reducing data movement and supporting repeated vegetation index computations in forest-monitoring workflows. The ICDE 2019 study~\cite{yu2019} further enhances these systems by proposing optimized storage layouts and spatiotemporal indexing strategies that improve data locality and I/O performance.

Unlike data-organization approaches such as the space-time cube, which primarily optimize indexing and historical retrieval, distributed frameworks like RDPro and Apache Sedona~\cite{yu2019} emphasize scalable execution and parallel processing. Together, these approaches demonstrate that both efficient data layout and distributed computation are necessary to support large-scale vegetation monitoring workflows. Our architecture builds directly on these principles: COG-formatted rasters on S3 provide cloud-native partial-read efficiency, PostGIS GIST indexes enable sub-second spatiotemporal queries over hundreds of scenes, and PySpark provides the distributed execution engine for pixel-level feature computation.
